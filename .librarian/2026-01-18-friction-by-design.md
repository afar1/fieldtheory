# Friction by Design

The 1960s telephone network had a peculiar safety feature: the second dial tone. When making a long-distance call, operators would pause and inject a confirmation tone—a deliberate interruption in the flow of connection. This wasn't a technical limitation; it was intentional friction designed to make users conscious of an expensive, irreversible action.

This principle, which interface designers would later call "speed bumps," appears throughout well-designed systems. Unix's `rm -rf /` requires special flags precisely because the command is too powerful to execute thoughtlessly. Git's push-force warns. Dangerous buttons are red.

The feedback confirmation modal serves the same function. Sharing something with Field Theory is a one-way door—a broadcast into a space you don't fully control. The modal transforms a keystroke into a conscious choice, turning muscle memory into mindful action.

There's an ongoing tension in interface design between flow and friction. Too much friction and users abandon ship; too little and they make regrettable decisions at machine speed. The art lies in placing the pause exactly where the stakes change—where local becomes public, where draft becomes permanent, where private becomes shared.

A well-placed confirmation dialog is less about preventing errors than about creating the psychological space for genuine intention.
