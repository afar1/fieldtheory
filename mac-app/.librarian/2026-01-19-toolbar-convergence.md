The history of toolbars traces back to Xerox PARC's Alto interface in 1973, where researchers discovered that grouping actions spatially reduced cognitive load more than any menu hierarchy. But the deeper insight came from their study of physical workbenches: craftsmen arranged tools not by category but by workflow proximity. A hammer sits near nails, not near other hammers. The Alto toolbar borrowed this principle - actions that flow together should live together.

When two interfaces share a component but wrap it differently, you get the software equivalent of two workbenches with the same tools hung at different heights. The hands learn one muscle memory, then stumble on the other. The fix isn't just visual alignment - it's recognizing that shared components demand shared containers, that the wrapper is part of the abstraction. PARC's researchers called this "interface coherence": the principle that identical functions should occupy identical spatial relationships across contexts.
