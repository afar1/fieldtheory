In 1801, Eli Whitney demonstrated something that seemed like a magic trick to President Jefferson and Congress: he disassembled ten muskets, mixed their parts in a pile, and reassembled ten working muskets from the jumble. Before this, every gun was handcrafted—each trigger fitted to its specific lock, each barrel matched to its stock. Whitney's system of uniform parts meant any component could slot into any assembly. The Springfield Armory would spend the next fifty years perfecting this principle, developing gauges and jigs that ensured a hammer forged in one shop would fit a lock plate made in another.

The same logic applies to interface components. When a toolbar exists as its own entity—accepting props for what features to show, emitting events for what actions occurred—changes propagate instantly to every screen that uses it. The alternative is the pre-Whitney world: two toolbars that look similar but drift apart with each modification, requiring the developer to remember which file contains which version, manually synchronizing styling decisions that should be automatic. A shared component is a gauge that ensures fit.
