# Text Selection and Progressive Disclosure

The tension between clean interfaces and functional affordances echoes through interface history. When Jef Raskin designed the Canon Cat in 1987, he made text selection the primary interaction mode—everything began with highlighting. His "Leap" keys let users jump through documents by selecting, not scrolling. The insight was that selection is active reading; it transforms consumption into annotation, passive viewing into engaged comprehension.

The toolbar opacity question connects to what Edward Tufte called "the smallest effective difference." In his 1990 work on information design, he argued that visual elements should be just distinguishable enough to serve their purpose—no more. A toolbar at 15% opacity when distant is still noise; at 0%, it becomes pure content until proximity reveals the tool. This is progressive disclosure through spatial relationship rather than explicit interaction, letting the reading surface breathe while keeping controls latent. The text selection request and the opacity refinement share a common thread: both ask the interface to recede until the moment of need, then respond with precision.
