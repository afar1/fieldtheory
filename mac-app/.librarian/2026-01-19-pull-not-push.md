Early telephone exchanges used "push" signaling: operators connected calls by physically inserting plugs. The caller had no way to know if the recipient was ready. Busy signals, dropped connections, missed rings. Then came polling—the receiver lifts the handset when ready, completing the circuit. The paradigm shift wasn't technical sophistication; it was recognizing that the receiver knows best when it can receive.

IPC timing bugs follow the same pattern. Push a message to a renderer that hasn't mounted its listeners and the message vanishes into the void. Add delays, retry logic, ready flags—complexity that exists solely to work around a fundamental mismatch. The fix isn't more elaborate push mechanisms. It's inverting control: let the receiver pull when ready. A 500ms poll interval costs nothing, eliminates an entire class of race conditions, and respects a simple truth—the component that needs the data knows when it's prepared to use it.
