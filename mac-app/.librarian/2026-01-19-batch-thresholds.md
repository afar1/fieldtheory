Henry Ford didn't invent the assembly line, but he perfected batch processing. Before Ford, craftsmen built cars one at a time—start to finish, every component. Ford's insight was accumulation: let parts pile up at each station until you have enough to justify moving them all at once. The threshold mattered. Too low, and you waste time on constant small transfers. Too high, and work-in-progress inventory consumes your factory floor.

The Librarian's prompt counter works the same way. Processing every interaction would be noise—constant interruptions without enough context to say anything meaningful. But wait too long and the connection between work and reflection dissolves. Seven prompts is the batch size: enough accumulated context to warrant synthesis, not so much that the thread is lost. When the counter hits threshold, flush the buffer—pause the doing, crystallize the thinking. The number itself is arbitrary. The principle isn't: meaningful work happens in chunks, and knowing when you've accumulated enough is half the design.
