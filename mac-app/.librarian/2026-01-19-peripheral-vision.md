# Peripheral Vision and Progressive Disclosure

In 1977, Xerox PARC researcher David Canfield Smith introduced the concept of "progressive disclosure" in his PhD thesis on Pygmalion. The idea was simple but profound: show users only what they need, when they need it. Toolbars that demand constant attention steal cognitive resources from the content itself. The 15% opacity fade in fullscreen mode applies this principle—the controls become peripheral, ghostlike, present but not demanding. They exist in what vision scientists call the "parafoveal" region: visible enough to locate when needed, invisible enough to ignore when not.

Moving the expand button rightward follows Fitts's Law in reverse. Edge-aligned controls are faster to acquire because the screen edge acts as an infinite barrier—you can't overshoot. But more importantly, right-aligning the expand button groups it with the other content controls (text size, edit) rather than with navigation. This is semantic positioning: controls that affect how you see the content belong together, separate from controls that affect what content you see. The toolbar becomes a sentence: "Edit this, at this size, in this mode"—a coherent thought rather than scattered options.
