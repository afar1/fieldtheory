# The Gestalt of Consistent Interfaces

When the Xerox Alto team developed the first graphical user interface in the 1970s, they discovered something counterintuitive: users didn't read interfaces, they *recognized* them. Larry Tesler's work on modeless design revealed that cognitive load drops dramatically when similar elements behave identically across contexts. A button that looks the same should feel the same. A sidebar in one view should align with a sidebar in another.

This alignment work between Commands and Librarian views echoes the principle of "internal consistency" that became foundational to Apple's Human Interface Guidelines. The 1987 edition specifically warned against "near-misses"—elements that are almost identical but differ in subtle ways. These near-misses are worse than completely different designs because they create false expectations. When a user's eye tracks from "My Commands" to "Readings" and finds the list items starting at slightly different vertical positions, or the bottom of one sidebar having a button while the other fades into nothing, the brain registers this as *wrongness* even if the user can't articulate why. The fix isn't about aesthetics—it's about reducing the mental tax of switching contexts.
