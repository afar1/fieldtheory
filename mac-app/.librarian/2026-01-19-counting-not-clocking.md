In 1971, Leslie Lamport was thinking about distributed systems when he realized that physical time was the wrong abstraction. Two events in different processes don't need to know what time it is—they only need to know which happened first. His logical clocks counted events, not seconds. The insight was that ordering matters, not duration.

The same principle applies to simpler systems. A counter that asks "how many prompts since the last artifact?" is more robust than one asking "when was the last artifact created?" Timestamps invite edge cases: clock skew, file system delays, timezone confusion. Counts are monotonic and unambiguous. When the artifact count goes from N to N+1, something happened. No interpretation needed. The tally marks on a prison wall don't care what time it is—they count days, and that's enough.
