# The Pavlovian Chime

The decision to add a discovery sound taps into one of the oldest tricks in behavioral psychology. Ivan Pavlov's 1897 experiments with dogs demonstrated that a neutral stimulus—a bell—could trigger salivation if consistently paired with food. The principle extends far beyond salivation: any reward, when reliably preceded by a signal, transfers some of its emotional weight to that signal. Slot machines exploit this with their carefully tuned win sounds. Video games have perfected it with the coin collect, the level-up fanfare, the achievement unlock. The ascending pitch of these sounds isn't arbitrary—it mirrors the prosodic contour of excited speech across all human languages.

What makes Field Theory's artifact sound different is what it signals: not a reward for consumption, but a reward for creation. The Librarian watches for new artifacts emerging from the user's work with AI. The chime doesn't celebrate buying, winning, or receiving—it celebrates thinking. Each time it plays, it reinforces a loop: collaborate with AI, produce insight, hear the chime, feel the small hit of discovery. Over time, the sound becomes associated not with passive receipt but with active synthesis. Pavlov's bell made dogs expect food; this bell teaches users to expect their own ideas, crystallized and returned to them in a form worth keeping.
